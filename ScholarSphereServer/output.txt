By now, you know WHERE the big clusters of people are, and this set of notes 
has a focus on the make-up of these populations. 
Why does it matter?
Political practices: Where people live has a large influence on how they vote. 
In the U.S., not only does the distribution of the population determine how 
many representatives each area gets, but where people live also is a good 
predictor of HOW they will vote. Rural areas (less densely populated) tend to 
vote more conservatively than more urban areas (densely population areas). 
Politicians in urban areas often campaign on promises of public services, 
infrastructure development, and other things that appeal to urban voters. Rural 
areas often don’t have these same needs and politicians campaign in a way 
that appeals to their wants and needs that differ from urban life.
Economic processes: population density is a very big determinant for where 
businesses will be located. Fewer businesses or jobs are located in rural 
areas, as there are not enough people in those places to support the 
businesses financially – you’ll learn much more about this pattern in our urban 
unit next semester.
2
Social Processes are also influenced by population distribution – The more 
students a school has, the more diversity and variety of classes the school is 
able to offer as there will be enough students for these different classes to 
make. Hospitals in large urban areas are able to have much more advanced 
medicine and diagnostic equipment available in large urban areas, while rural-
area hospitals tend to be only equipped to handle minor-emergencies or 
routine care, such as broken bones. People in rural areas who experience life-
threatening emergencies are often rushed into the nearest large city so they 
can get the level of care they need, even if it means passing several smaller 
hospitals along the way. 
Knowing the composition of a population--at all scales of analysis, from 
neighborhoods to world regions—helps us understand each place in context of 
the people who live there. This class is all about the people, after all.

There are three component parts to every population pyramid: The age 
cohorts (groups of people within the same age group), the vertical axis (age 
ranges), and the horizontal axis, which either displays the total number of the 
population or the percentage of the population. 
The sexes are separated in population pyramids – males will be on one side, 
and females will be on the other. On the pyramid above, the males are on the 
left, and the females are on the right. Most of the time, these things are in 
color and will use different colors, but if it’s not, check the labels. 
• If it is not labeled, look at the very top layers of the pyramid to see which 
side has more people – that will be the female side. (this is a normal pattern 
that exists across most societies– women, on average, tend to have a 
longer life expectancy).
Age may be on the side or the middle but is always the vertical axis. 
Age Cohorts are displayed horizontally, stacked from youngest to oldest. 
Refer to the Age axis to determine the size of the cohort (age group). The most 
common cohort size is 5 years. This means every person within that cohort will 
have been born within the same 5 year time period (example: 2001-2005, 
2006-2010, etc.)

When we look at the age structure of the population, we’re really looking at the 
ratio between the oldest 1/3 and the working age population (between 15 and 
60), or the youngest 1/3 and the working age group. We call this the 
dependency ratio (more on next slide)
6
The Dependency Ratio is the balance between the “bearing bracket” – those 
of working age, 15-60ish, who “bear” the economic, social, and political burden 
of the society. 
The bearing bracket must: 
• Care for the children
• Provide the income for their families
• Pay into their employer’s retirement system to provide salaries and 
healthcare for retirees.
• Pay the vast majority of taxes to the government for government services 
such as infrastructure, government operating costs, welfare/social security, 
military expenditures, etc.
• Care for their elderly parents
If there is a significant unbalance – many more children than adults or many 
more older adults than working age or children, there are going to be social, 
economic, and political issues associated with that pattern (more discussion 
on in the notes “Aging populations – coming soon to a website near you).

When we look at the ratio between the men and women, we’re looking for 
evenness. If the ratio is uneven, we want to know WHY . Geographers get a 
clue to this WHY when we look at WHERE it is occurring. 
Examples: Some cultures, such as China, have a well-known preference for 
male children. When the Chinese government put restrictions on the number 
of children families can have, a significant imbalance of male-to-female 
children resulted.
Other places, such as Qatar, an oil-rich nation of Southwest Asia, is 
experiencing rapid economic growth. They do not have the population of adults 
to meet the demands of the developers (construction, especially), so they 
bring in “guest workers” from other countries, primarily places like India. These 
guest workers are virtually all single males (single women are not permitted to 
be guest workers in construction). This will cause a significant skew on the 
population pyramid. 
If we zoom in to a city, there may be a large imbalance caused by a military 
base (mostly males between ages 18-40).

growth. Is there a good balance in the dependency ratio?
If the answer is no, the shape of the pyramid gives us a clue –
Youthful populations are often socially and economically underdeveloped –
there may be a lack of healthcare, limited access to education, and limited 
access to sanitation. A large percentage of the economy likely works in the 
primary sector of the economy (agriculture). Economically, there may not be 
enough jobs for the kids as they become adults, and there is likely a rapid 
rural-to-urban migration. 
Aged population often have too much burden on the bearing bracket for social 
services such as healthcare for the elderly (hospitals and nursing homes), as 
well as economic issues such as not enough people to fill jobs, which can 
result in economic instability and companies moving their businesses to other 
countries.

Population momentum is the time period that follows a country’s TFR drop to 
replacement rate – there are still a large number of people in the population 
who continue to grow, so even if each if the early adults are only replacing 
themselves, the population continues to grow as they “age out” beyond the 
age of reproduction. It takes several decades after TFR reaches replacement 
rate for the population growth to level off. 
Example – China instituted a “One Child” policy in the 1979. The TFR of China 
quickly fell to replacement rate over the next decade. However, even today, 
the population of China continues to grow as all the children who were born in 
the 1970s and early 1980s are just now reaching middle age, and their 
children have not yet reached the age to start their own families. China is still 
in the period of population momentum.
China’s period of population momentum is actually short compared to those 
countries that go through the transition organically (without outside influence 
from government or other). Many parts of India have lowered their TRF to near 
replacement rates, but the country is expected to continue to grow until the 
population pyramid “fills out” – in other words, it completes the transition from 
steep-sided pyramid to box, which will take several generations. 
17
“peak child” is another way to say replacement rate – what that means is that 
when an area hits “peak child” it has the maximum total number of children 
that there will ever be in this society. All generations before had fewer total 
children, and future generations will have fewer children as well because the 
adults are having smaller families. As a whole, the world hit “peak child” 
around the year 2000. 
The phenomena of population momentum is why demographers can estimate 
that the world population will stop growing at around 11 billion people.

This population pyramid of China in 1982 shows several distinct periods in 
Chinese history. 
The big gap in the cohort that was born in 1958-1962 shows a tremendous dip 
in the population – this is during what was known as the “Great Leap Forward” 
which was actually an attempt by the communist government of China with the 
goal of collectivization of agricultural and industrial output that led to a famine 
that left as many as 45 million dead. This dip in the population on the pyramid 
shows both a drop in the number of births, but also a spike in infant mortality 
rates during that time. 
Following the Great Leap Forward, there was a baby boom (huge increase in 
births), where the pyramid bulges outward. 
Then, in 1979, China instituted it’s One Child Policy to curb the rapid 
population growth by limiting families to have only one child (enforced mostly 
in cities through tax penalties and denying subsequent children access to 
services such as school and healthcare). As you can see on the graph, the 
age cohorts gets smaller and smaller.
